Detecting Twelve Knee Abnormalities from a Single MRI Study

A knee MRI study is not one picture but several stacks of slices, taken in different planes. My models read short stacks of neighbouring slices, let an attention layer decide which ones matter for each finding, and output twelve probabilities — from ACL and meniscus tears to effusion and fractures. This page walks through every step, with the numbers behind each decision.

01Problem

One study, several series, twelve noisy targets

The input is not a single image. Each study holds several MRI series — stacks of 20–40 slices taken in a given plane with a given contrast. The output is twelve independent probabilities, scored by ROC AUC per finding and averaged with equal weight.

1.1What a study looks like

I reduce every study to five fixed "slots". Fluid-sensitive sequences make swelling and tears bright; the others show anatomy. Not every study has all five, so a missing slot stays empty and is masked — another sequence is never substituted into it.

1.3Where the labels come from

This is the harder part. Only 58 of the 4,407 training studies were labelled by radiologists. Everything else comes with a free-text report, from which participants extracted labels with language models. The task is therefore weakly supervised: validation measures agreement with report-derived labels, not clinical accuracy.

Figure 2Label sources in the training set. The 58 expert-labelled studies are a sliver — and I kept them out of every decision.
Scope. This is competition research, not a diagnostic tool. Cross-validation, the public leaderboard and the hidden run are three different kinds of evidence, and I report them separately.
02Data & labels

Fix the rules before looking at scores

Before training anything, I froze three things: which label set to learn from, how studies are split into folds, and how the score is computed. Every later experiment reused them, so any difference in score comes from the model — not from a lucky split.

2.1Soft labels from six label sets

Six public, CC0-licensed label sets were available, each extracted from the reports by a different participant. Instead of picking one, I average them. If four of six say "ACL tear", the target is 0.67 — the model learns that the report is ambiguous rather than being forced to a hard yes or no. Sets that duplicated another or were tuned on the 58 expert studies were left out.

Figure 3How a soft label is formed for one study and one finding. Click a dot to flip that vote and see the target change. ILLUSTRATION

2.2Five frozen folds

The 4,258 studies used for training are split once into five folds of 851–852 studies. Each model is trained five times, each time holding out one fold for validation, so every study is predicted exactly once by a model that never saw it.

03Pipeline

From slice stacks to a study-level prediction

A 3D network on full MRI volumes is slow to train and data-hungry. Instead I use a "2.5D" approach: standard 2D image networks that see three neighbouring slices at once, followed by an attention layer that decides which slices matter. Four steps turn a raw study into twelve probabilities.

3.1Crop to the same physical size

Scanners use different pixel sizes, so the same knee can fill 280 or 470 pixels. I crop every slice to a fixed 140 mm field using the pixel spacing stored in the DICOM header, then resize to 384 px. Now one pixel means roughly the same physical distance in every study.

3.2Stack three neighbouring slices

Image networks expect three colour channels. I fill them with three adjacent slices (z−1, z, z+1) instead of red, green and blue. The network then sees a little depth — enough to tell a tear running across slices from noise on one slice — at the cost of a normal 2D model. A study yields up to 86 such stacks across its five slots.

3.3Let each finding choose its slices

The backbone turns every stack into a feature vector. A study-level label does not say which slice shows the tear, so this is a multiple-instance problem: I learn a separate attention layer per finding that weights the stacks before pooling. An ACL tear can focus on a few central sagittal slices, while osteoarthritis spreads its attention over coronal slices.

3.4Average a mirrored view

At test time every study is also predicted as its anatomical mirror image, and the two predictions are averaged. The mirror has to be correct for each plane: for sagittal stacks, left–right is the slice order, so the three channels are reversed; for coronal and axial, the image is flipped horizontally.

04Models

Three model families, one recipe

All families share the labels, folds, loss (soft binary cross-entropy), optimiser (AdamW, 5% warm-up, cosine decay) and attention head. They differ in what they see and how they are trained — on purpose, so that their mistakes differ too.

5.3What F adds

Comparing the three-model ensemble against A + T shows where the gain comes from: F helps on 9 of 12 findings — most on fracture (+0.010), then MCL and medial meniscus — and slightly hurts ACL and medial OA. That matches where its errors differ most from A (Figure 13).

Figure 12AUC change per finding when F is added to A + T (folds 0–1).

5.4How different are the mistakes?

To judge diversity I compare prediction errors: the Spearman correlation of residuals (prediction minus label) between two families. 1.0 would mean identical mistakes. F is most different on fracture and MCL; the experimental family G was more different everywhere — but, as section 08 shows, that did not translate into enough gain.

Figure 13Error correlation with family A per finding (mean of folds 0–1). Further left = more complementary.
An honest footnote on F. F had to pass two thresholds set before training: an ensemble gain of at least +0.002 (it reached +0.0027) and an average error correlation with A of at most 0.91 (it was 0.925). I kept it after seeing the result, so its inclusion is a judgement call, not a pre-registered pass.
06Final blend

Standing on a public ensemble — and saying so

By the last week, the strongest public notebooks scored 0.958 and hundreds of teams were submitting identical copies. Copies all receive the same private score. My final submission blends my own models into one of them (goodpjw2008, Apache-2.0), which I did not train, so it can move away from the crowd if my models carry real signal.

6.1Blend ranks, not probabilities

AUC only cares about the order of predictions, and two models can be calibrated very differently. So I convert each model's predictions to ranks per finding and mix the ranks:

final = 0.90 · rank(public ensemble) + 0.10 · rank(own ensemble)

Drag the slider to see how the own model's weight reorders six example studies. At small weights it only breaks near-ties in the public model — exactly where a second opinion is most useful.

No medal. Every submission dropped by 0.016–0.019 from public to private. The blend scored the same as the public ensemble alone to three decimals, so my models at 10% weight neither helped nor hurt: a 0.928 model added to a 0.942 one did not see enough of what the stronger model missed. Differentiating from public copies only works when your own model is competitive on its own — which, for this task, meant anatomy-guided crops built early.

07Hidden run

A model only counts if it finishes

↳

Coming from the ensemble: adding F improved validation — but the first two submissions containing F crashed on the hidden test set, which returns no error log.

A full rehearsal on 1,300 training studies ran cleanly, so I could not reproduce the crash. Instead of guessing the cause, I made every failure path I could think of survivable, then forced each one on purpose in a 100-study test.

Figure 16Robust inference. Every branch was triggered deliberately in testing before the real submission.
  1. Check every output
    Logits are tested for NaN or infinity after every forward pass, for every fold and every mirrored view, before anything is averaged.
  2. Retry in full precision
    A pass that overflows in half precision is re-run in fp32, in chunks of 16 stacks so the retry cannot run out of GPU memory (peak 6.5 GB).
  3. Fall back instead of failing
    If F still fails on a study, that study uses A + T; if all own models fail, the training prevalence. The submission file is checked — every row, every target, all finite — and written atomically.
Result. The next submission ran end to end and scored 0.947 with no external data. The rehearsal took 4 h 50 min on two T4 GPUs for 1,300 studies.
Mirror check. An outside reviewer asked whether the test-time mirror flips sagittal slices front-to-back. It does not: sagittal stacks are reversed in slice order and other planes are flipped left–right (Figure 9).

The one I would start with next time

The strongest public single models zoom into the menisci and cartilage using an anatomy network. Retraining that pipeline on my labels looked like the biggest remaining gain, but it needed raw DICOMs (253.6 GB compressed) instead of my cached slices. Inference alone measured 13.3 s per study on two T4 GPUs, and the original author reported ~15 hours of training. I stopped it with two days left rather than put the final submissions at risk.

09Lessons

What I would do differently

Measure the ensemble, not the model

A weaker model that makes different mistakes can be worth more than a stronger copy of what you already have (Figures 10 and 12).

Build the data path first

Raw-data access and anatomy-guided crops belong in week one. In the final week, data movement — not GPU time — was the bottleneck.

Read the strongest public work early

Several ideas I reached late were already described in public write-ups.

Treat the hidden run as part of the model

Force every fallback on purpose. A crash scores zero, however good the model is.

Hypothesis for future work: local, anatomy-guided cropping is probably the biggest gap between my models and the strongest public ones. It was not measured here.
